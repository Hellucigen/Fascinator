Embodied agents accumulate experience far faster than they can write it back as persistent state: action histories remain logs. We present Fascinator (FAS), a cognitive-architecture prototype that keeps semantic, episodic, and procedural content on a single typed, weighted graph and writes experience back as relational structure through one evidence-weighted attribution pipeline. The paper contributes an auditable memory architecture with a preregistered evaluation protocol that delimits when spreading activation adds value over flat retrieval. In an information-asymmetric beacon task, persistent graph context yields 20/20 completions versus 0/20 without context (McNemar p=2e-6; the 0/20 is a built-in existence verification); an activation-free embedding-retrieval control reproduces the full benefit (20/20). A context-removal experiment establishes that persistence itself is causally operative: after graph reset, goal resumption drops from 10/10 to 0/10 (exact McNemar p=0.00195, the minimum attainable at n=10) and failure repetitions on an invalidated goal rise from 2.8 to 7.7 (one-sided Wilcoxon p=0.00098); whenever the experience stream remains re-readable, baselines match FAS on resumption and exceed it on failure repetition (0.0 vs 2.8), so the causal role attaches to persistence itself. A preregistered mechanism campaign (4x50 paired seeds) rejects activation's incremental value over a flat baseline (Delta-JCG<0, Holm p=1.5e-9, 0/200 reversals), isolating one exception: multi-input convergence through a shared hub (30/30 vs 0/30); an 84-cell sweep maps it to weak edges and shallow paths. A second campaign (508 archived runs) measures the trade-off directly: FAS resumes an abandoned goal in 9/10 runs (baselines 0/10) but repeats an invalidated goal 10.5 times vs the baseline's 1.0. All negative results, assembly audits, and the full protocol are reported.
